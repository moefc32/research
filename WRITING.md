# Article Drafting Guideline

## Purpose

Use this guideline to prepare a Research subsite article from a research project, study, evaluation, experiment, or research-informed investigation. The preliminary questions are intended to capture enough of the real story for a coherent working draft without inventing findings or overstating the research.

Research subsite theme: **Published Works**

The article should make research understandable as a real investigation. It should explain why the research was conducted, what was examined, how the approach was used, what was found, what the findings actually support, and what remains outside the study's scope.

These articles are written for a general audience. They should remain accessible without stripping away the research logic.

---

## Part 1: Preliminary Questions

Answer these questions, focus on the investigation and what it contributed, rather than trying to reproduce the structure of an academic paper :

1. **What question emerged from the situation?**  
   Describe what you noticed, why it raised a question worth investigating, and what you wanted the research to clarify.

2. **How did you go about finding the answer?**  
   Describe the subjects, evidence, sources, participants, methods, or instruments you used, along with the important choices that shaped the investigation.

3. **What did the evidence tell you?**  
   Describe the findings, patterns, contradictions, surprises, or results that emerged, and how they affected your understanding of the original question.

4. **What remained after the research?**  
   Describe what the research contributed, how it was used or published, what it could not establish, and what questions or possibilities remained open.

---

## Part 2: Editorial Drafting

Use the completed preliminary answers as the foundational source to assemble the working draft following this editorial instruction :

```md
Draft a working article for the **Research** subsite, whose theme is **Published Works**.

The source material below contains my actual research story. Treat it as factual source material. Do not invent methods, participants, data, findings, motivations, implementation, or conclusions that are not supported by it. Do not infer stronger claims than the research can support. If something is uncertain or outside the study's scope, preserve that boundary.

The article should translate the research into an accessible story for a general audience. It should explain why I conducted the research, what I wanted to understand, how I approached the investigation, what I found, and what happened afterward. The research itself is the foundation, but the article should also make clear why the work mattered in its original context.

Preserve my personal motivation and internal reasoning. Do not turn the article into a conventional academic paper or simply summarize the abstract, methodology, results, and conclusion. The reader should understand the practical situation that made the research worth doing.

Make the scope of the research explicit where necessary. Distinguish between what the study demonstrated, what I inferred from it, and what remains unknown. If the research produced a recommendation, prototype, adjustment, or later project, describe it as an outcome of the research without overstating its validation.

Do not force a retrospective design-process narrative into the article. The Research subsite is about the research and what it revealed. Deeper reasoning about design decisions belongs more naturally to the Design subsite when the source material supports that distinction.

Build a clear article structure with useful section headings. Avoid a generic academic sequence if a more natural storytelling structure better represents the actual investigation.

The final section should close the storytelling without pretending that the research itself is completely closed. It can acknowledge limitations, unexplored areas, later uncertainty, or what the research could not determine. A rhetorical question may be used only when it naturally emerges from this specific research story. Do not use one simply because the article needs a dramatic ending.

Writing constraints :

- Use first person where appropriate.
- Preserve my personal motivation and reasoning.
- Every paragraph must contain multiple sentences and develop a complete thought. Never use a single-sentence paragraph.
- Keep the language accessible to general readers while retaining necessary research terminology.
- Do not overstate scientific certainty.
- Distinguish findings from interpretation and later reflection.
- Avoid unnecessary rhetorical questions.
- Do not use em dashes.
- Do not use inline colons or semicolons.
- Avoid generic academic filler and phrases that make the article sound like a paper being mechanically simplified.
- Target approximately 650–1000 words unless the source material clearly requires a different length.
- Do not use a "Conclusion" heading. The final section should have a meaningful title appropriate to the specific story.

After drafting, check the article for factual fidelity, unsupported claims, repeated context, excessive methodology detail, missing scope boundaries, and places where the research has been made to sound more conclusive than it actually was.

```
